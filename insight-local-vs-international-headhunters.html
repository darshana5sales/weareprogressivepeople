For Candidates

Local or international? Choosing an executive search partner.

By Scott Deutrom 5 February 2026 6 min read
On this page
  • What local really buys you
  • When reach genuinely matters
  • Distinguish the firm's footprint from the role's remit
  • Questions worth asking

“Headhunters near me” is one of the more revealing things people search for, because proximity is usually a proxy for something else: the wish to deal with someone who actually knows the market you work in, and who will still be reachable in two years.

Physical location matters less than it once did. Market location matters more.

What local really buys you

A search partner embedded in your market has four advantages that no amount of international footprint substitutes for:

  • They know who moved, and why. Not from a database — from conversations. In a small senior market that context is the whole job.
  • They know what things actually pay. Local, current compensation reality rather than a global band.
  • They are accountable. A firm that works one market cannot afford to treat you badly; you will meet them again.
  • They can read the business. Whether a client is genuinely funded, whether the last person in that seat left for a good reason, whether the board is aligned.

For UK-based senior leaders in advanced media, this is decisive. The market is small enough that the difference between a partner who is in it and one who covers it from a distance shows up in the first conversation.

When reach genuinely matters

There are real cases for an internationally structured firm: a simultaneous leadership build-out across several territories with local employment complexity; a role that must be filled from a genuinely global pool because the experience exists in only a handful of places; or a board search where an investor expects a particular brand of process.

Outside those, international reach is frequently sold and rarely used. Most senior UK and EMEA advanced-media appointments are filled from a population that a well-networked local specialist already knows personally.

Most senior EMEA advanced-media appointments are filled from a population a well-networked local specialist already knows by name.

Distinguish the firm's footprint from the role's remit

This is the distinction that gets muddled most often, and it matters to candidates in particular.

A search practice being UK-based says something about where its network is deepest. It says nothing about the scope of the roles it works on. Plenty of the mandates we run are for UK-based leaders whose remit covers EMEA or is genuinely global — and the two facts are entirely compatible.

What you should be sceptical of is the opposite claim: a firm that presents itself as operating everywhere. Ask where they have actually placed someone in the last year, and the answer usually resolves to two or three markets.

Questions worth asking

  1. Which roles have you placed in my market in the last twelve months, at what level?
  2. Who, by name, will be my point of contact — and will that still be true in six months?
  3. How do you handle a role whose remit crosses markets you do not personally cover?
  4. What is your off-limits position, and does it affect roles I might be interested in?
  5. If nothing suitable comes up for a year, what does our contact look like?

The last one is the best question a candidate can ask. The answer tells you whether you are being recruited or being catalogued.
