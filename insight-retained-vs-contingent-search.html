Retained vs. contingent headhunting: when each model actually works.

By Keith Rattray 29 January 2026 7 min read
On this page

  • When retained is clearly right
  • When contingent genuinely works
  • The failure mode in the middle
  • Questions to settle before you engage
  • Where we land

Most search firms argue for one model because it is the one they sell. Retained-only firms present contingency as amateur. Contingency firms present retainers as paying upfront for work that might not happen. Both positions are commercially motivated and neither is much use if you are trying to decide how to fill a specific role.

Here is the version we would give a client who asked us straight.

What each model actually funds

The difference is not fee level. It is what the fee structure makes economically rational for the firm doing the work.

Retained search is paid in stages regardless of outcome — typically a third at engagement, a third at shortlist, a third at placement. That structure funds work with no guaranteed return: mapping a market exhaustively, approaching people who are not looking, running long structured interviews, triangulating references beyond the names offered, and managing an offer over weeks.

Contingent search is paid only on placement. That makes speed to a placeable candidate the rational priority, and it makes deep exploratory work irrational — a firm cannot spend three weeks mapping a market for a role it has a one-in-four chance of filling.

The difference between the models is not the fee. It is what the fee structure makes it rational for the search firm to spend time on.

When retained is clearly right

  • Confidential searches. Replacing an incumbent who does not know, or hiring ahead of an announcement. Contingency cannot run discreetly at scale.
  • Genuinely scarce profiles. Where fewer than thirty people worldwide qualify, and most are not looking.
  • Board and C-suite appointments. The assessment depth and reference rigour required are not economically rational on contingency.
  • Where the brief needs reframing. If the role definition is still forming, you are buying advisory work as much as search.
  • Where a failed hire is expensive. Which, for revenue leadership, it almost always is.

When contingent genuinely works

  • Well-defined management roles in a liquid part of the market, where qualified candidates are numerous and reachable.
  • Volume or repeat hiring into an established team profile.
  • Where speed matters more than exhaustiveness and a good hire beats a perfect one.
  • Where you have your own strong pipeline and want a supplementary channel rather than a full process.

There is nothing second-rate about this. Insisting on a retainer for a role that does not need one is not rigour, it is billing.

The failure mode in the middle

The genuinely poor outcome is a senior, confidential or scarce-profile role run on contingency, usually by three firms at once. What follows is predictable: each firm rushes the candidates it already knows, the same person is submitted twice, the market learns the role is open and being shopped, and the strongest passive candidates are never approached because nobody could justify the time.

If a role has retained characteristics, running it on contingency does not save the fee. It usually costs a quarter.

Questions to settle before you engage

  1. How many genuinely qualified people exist, and how many are reachable without a direct approach?
  2. Does this need to be confidential, at any stage?
  3. Is the brief settled, or does it need pressure-testing first?
  4. What does a failed hire cost us in time and revenue?
  5. Would we be comfortable if this role were visibly in the market?

Two or more answers pointing at scarcity, confidentiality or cost of failure means retained. Otherwise, contingent is a reasonable, often better, choice.

Where we land

Progressive People works on both a retained and contingent basis, and we match the engagement to the brief rather than prescribing every client to the same model. Practically, that means we will tell you when a role does not need a retainer — including when that costs us fee, because a client who is told straight once tends to come back.

If you would like a view on which model your next search actually needs, brief our team.
