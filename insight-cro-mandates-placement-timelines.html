For Employers

CRO mandates: what really drives a six-month placement vs. an eighteen-month miss.

By Keith Rattray 5 March 2026 6 min read
On this page

  • Testing the compensation envelope before the search
  • Being honest about what the CRO inherits
  • Running a process the candidate can actually survive
  • What good looks like

Almost every Chief Revenue Officer search we are asked to review has the same problem, and it is almost never the one the client thinks it is. The client believes the market is thin. In most cases the market is fine; the brief is not.

Over the searches we have run and the failed processes we have been asked to rescue, the difference between a CRO placement that completes in six months and one that drifts past eighteen comes down to four decisions, all of which are made before a single candidate is approached.

1. Deciding which job it actually is

“Chief Revenue Officer” describes at least four distinct jobs, and they attract different people.

  • The builder. Founder-led selling needs converting into a repeatable go-to-market engine. The hire is really about process, hiring and forecasting discipline.
  • The scaler. The engine exists and needs to run harder, usually into new territories or segments.
  • The broadener. A single-product revenue line needs turning into a platform proposition, often requiring the commercial narrative to be rewritten.
  • The fixer. Revenue has stalled or churn has spiked, and the first two quarters are diagnostic.

A brief that describes all four is describing none of them. Candidates read it as a business that has not decided what it needs, and the strongest ones — who are in role, well-compensated and not looking — decline the first conversation.

A brief that describes four jobs at once is read by the market as a business that has not decided what it needs.

2. Testing the compensation envelope before the search, not after

The most common single cause of an eighteen-month CRO search is a comp envelope set against last year's benchmark and a candidate profile set against this year's ambition.

It surfaces late and expensively: a process runs to final stage, the offer lands ten to fifteen per cent below the market, the candidate declines, and the search restarts having burned four months and told a dozen senior people in a small industry that the business could not close.

The fix is unglamorous. Before approaching anyone, agree the realistic total package — base, variable, equity and the vesting reality behind it — for the specific profile described, and agree what happens if the right person sits above it. A search partner who will not give you a straight benchmark at the brief stage is not going to give you a straight read on a candidate later either.

3. Being honest about what the CRO inherits

Senior commercial candidates diligence the job properly. They will ask about the state of the team, the quality of the pipeline data, whether marketing reports in, what the churn profile looks like, and how much of last year's number came from two accounts.

Briefs that gloss over the difficult parts do not avoid the conversation, they simply move it to week six of the process, where it is far more likely to end the conversation. Stated up front, most of those problems are the reason a strong candidate finds the job interesting — a turnaround is a career story, a surprise is a red flag.

4. Running a process the candidate can actually survive

A CRO candidate in role is running their own quarter while interviewing. Processes that require six separate conversations across ten weeks, with two-week gaps and a case study delivered over a weekend, lose people to less demanding processes elsewhere — not because they were not interested, but because the timetable told them how the business makes decisions.

The processes that close fast tend to share a shape:

  • A founder or CEO conversation early, not as a final gate.
  • Three to four stages, scheduled in advance as a block rather than arranged one at a time.
  • A commercial working session instead of an abstract case study — a real account, a real pricing problem.
  • Board or investor exposure at the right point, prepared rather than improvised.
  • An offer conversation that has already been rehearsed with the search partner.

What good looks like

For a well-defined senior commercial mandate with an agreed comp envelope, a realistic shape is a longlist in three to four weeks, a shortlist at six to eight, offer at ten to fourteen, and a start date governed by notice period. Highly confidential replacements and searches carrying multi-market scope extend that, sometimes to twenty weeks.

What does not extend it, in our experience, is market scarcity. It is nearly always the brief.
