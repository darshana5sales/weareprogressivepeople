For Candidates

How to find a headhunter who can actually help your career.

By Scott Deutrom 26 February 2026 5 min read
On this page
  • Find the two or three who actually cover your market
  • Make contact when you do not need anything
  • What to share
  • Signals worth paying attention to
  • Play the long game

Most senior leaders meet headhunters the wrong way round: a call arrives about a specific role, the timing is wrong, and the relationship never develops past a polite decline. Two years later, when the timing is right, there is no one to call.

The people who get the most value from headhunters treat the relationship as something built in advance, not something activated in a crisis. Here is what that looks like in practice.

Find the two or three who actually cover your market

The instinct is to talk to as many recruiters as possible. It is the wrong instinct. Senior markets are small, and being visibly available to everyone devalues you — particularly if the same profile reaches a hiring CEO from four different sources.

Instead, identify the two or three firms that genuinely specialise in your discipline and seniority. Test them the way a client would: can they talk about your market with real fluency? Do they know who moved where in the last year, and why? A specialist will be discussing the market within five minutes. A generalist will be discussing their process.

Make contact when you do not need anything

The best time to meet a headhunter is when you are settled and have nothing to ask for. The conversation is better, more honest and more useful on both sides — and it means that when something does change, you are a known quantity rather than a CV.

It also means you are the person a headhunter calls first when a mandate lands that fits, which is where most genuinely interesting senior moves come from.

What to share, and what you are entitled to ask

A good headhunter can only represent you well with real information. That means career narrative rather than job titles, the numbers you have actually carried and delivered, what you want next and — just as importantly — what you will not do. Be specific about compensation expectations; vagueness here wastes everyone's quarter.

In return, you are entitled to expect:

  • Confidentiality by default. Your details should never go to a client without your explicit agreement, on a named basis.
  • A real brief. Who the client is, what the job genuinely involves, what the comp envelope is, and why the role is open.
  • Honest feedback. Including when you are not right for something, and why.
  • To be told when nothing is happening. Silence is the most common complaint senior candidates have about search firms, and it is entirely avoidable.

Signals worth paying attention to

Some behaviours reliably predict a poor experience:

  • A role described without naming the client, more than one conversation in.
  • Pressure to agree to a submission before you have seen a proper brief.
  • A CV request before any conversation about what you actually want.
  • An inability to answer basic questions about the hiring manager or the reason for the vacancy.
  • Volume outreach — the same role sent to you and four peers in the same week.

Play the long game

The senior advanced-media market is small enough that reputation compounds in both directions. Headhunters remember the people who took a call when they had nothing to gain, gave a straight answer, or made a useful introduction. Those are the people who hear about the mandate that is not advertised.

If you lead revenue, product, supply or operations in AdTech, advanced TV, publishing or MarTech and would like to be on our radar, you can share your profile confidentially. We will not submit it anywhere without speaking to you first.
